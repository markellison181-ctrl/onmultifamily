**Refresher: Capital Gains Simplified**

Capital gain is the profit made when an asset, like property, is sold for more than its purchase price. In Canada, the capital gains inclusion rate for corporations is 50%, meaning half of the capital gain is taxable.

For example, if a corporation buys a property for $10 million and sells it for $20 million, the capital gain is $10 million. With a 50% inclusion rate, $5 million of this gain is taxable. This amount is then taxed at the corporate tax rate (note this example does not account for depreciation, which would increase the taxable amount).

**History of Capital Gains (Inclusion Rate)**

To understand what might could happen. It's important to understand the history of the inclusion rate in Canada.

It was originally introduced in 1972 at 50%. 

- Then, increased to 66.67% in 1988 and further to 75% in 1990. 

- In 2000, the rate was reduced back to 50%, where it has remained since.

**How Does Canada Compare to G7 Nations**

Canada is among the least competitive. Here's how we stack up against other countries.

- **Canada:** We see the fluctuations as discussed earlier, with the current rate at 50%.

- **USA:** The rate has varied, with a decrease over time, currently standing at 20%.

- **UK:** The rate increased initially and then decreased, currently at 20%.

- **France:** Started from no capital gains tax in 1972 and gradually increased to 30%.

- **Germany:** No capital gains tax until 2000, then a significant rise to 60%.

- **Italy:** Fluctuated over the years, currently at 26%.

- **Japan:** Similar to the USA, with a decrease over time to the current rate of 20%.

**What You Should Care About:**

Major tax changes, including those affecting capital gains, would typically be announced during the federal budget release. This is usually in the spring (February to April) but can vary.

Governments rarely apply tax changes retroactively, so if anything is to happen, you would have a few months to plan.
